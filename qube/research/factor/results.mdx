Open a completed, successful analysis and check its dates, stock pool, and settings. The result belongs to that run, even if you have edited the factor since then.

## Start with the groups

Groups are ordered from low factor scores to high scores. With five groups, group 1 contains the lowest scores and group 5 the highest. Positive direction targets the highest group; negative direction targets the lowest.

Ask: **Does the pattern match the idea?** If your idea says higher scores should perform better, you would look for higher-score groups to do better consistently. If only the middle group does well, or the ordering reverses across periods, the simple explanation may not hold.

The **long-short** series compares the two ends of the ranking. With positive direction it is highest group minus lowest; with negative direction it is lowest minus highest. `long_short_2` compares the second-highest and second-lowest groups in the chosen direction. These comparisons are research series, not proof of a directly executable trade.

## Check the experience along the way

| Figure                      | What it tells you                                         | What it does not tell you alone                                       |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Total and annualized return | Overall performance and an equivalent yearly growth rate. | Whether the pattern is stable or executable after your trading costs. |
| Maximum drawdown            | The largest fall from a previous high.                    | How long recovery took or what future losses could be.                |
| Sharpe ratio                | Return relative to variability.                           | Why the signal works or whether it will persist.                      |

A high final return can hide long losing periods. Look at the path of the group curves, not just the ending number. Ask whether a few dates or stocks explain most of the result, how much data was missing, and how often the group membership changed.

## Then inspect the signal diagnostics

<strong>IC</strong>, short for information coefficient, measures the relationship between the score
and later returns. <strong>Rank IC</strong> compares their rankings instead of their raw values. A
positive relationship means higher scores tended to go with higher later returns in the measured
sample; a negative one points the other way.

Do not judge the factor from the average IC alone. Ask Qube to show whether the relationship changes sign over time and whether it survives a separate period. A small sample or strongly related observations can make statistical figures look more convincing than they are.

<Accordion title="Metric names in exported results">
  `IC_mean`, `IC_std`, and `IC_IR` describe the mean, dispersion, and mean-to-variability ratio of Pearson information coefficients. `Rank_IC` describes the rank relationship. `IR` is the information ratio of the selected return series; it is distinct from IC diagnostics. Review sample size and dependence when reading `t-stat` and IC probability statistics.

Preview curves may be sampled. Count trading observations from the actual result, not the number of preview points. `metrics.rows` counts input rows, not unique stocks. Latest factor values may be standardized scores rather than raw prices.

</Accordion>

## Decide what to investigate next

If the result looks useful, check a separate period, explain why the signal might exist, and inspect missing data. Preserve a [named version](/qube/research/factor/versions) of a useful successful analysis. To test actual buying, selling, amounts, and costs, define a [strategy](/qube/research/strategy/factor-to-strategy).
